Writing Sample #1

Small Steps to Optimal Financial Health

Written by: Joshua J. Lavallais


Disclaimer: the purpose of this article is not to humiliate or shame individuals under financial duress. Instead, the intent is to identify common fiscal mistakes and induce good financial health via suggested principles found within this text.


There is a nexus between good financial health and one's overall health. When the human body is under attack, bad health manifests in ailments such as stress or dysfunction. Concerning our wallets, without good financial health, the same ailments apply. In June of 2024, a Market Watch survey revealed that 64% of Americans reported financial problems as their biggest source of stress.

In an August 2023 CNBC article, columnist Jessica Dickler reported that millionaires – yes, millionaires – also feel financially insecure. The idea that seven figures and up aren’t sufficient to stave off monetary concerns is somewhat mindboggling. Yet one must recognize a few things:


1. (PV) The Present Value of a million dollars isn’t what it was decades ago, thanks to inflation.

2. About inflation – since 2021, we’ve seen the highest hikes in interest rates since 1981.

3. Our economy and job markets are still reeling from the byproducts of Covid-19, such as lockdowns and inflation.


Consequently, millionaires, the middle class, and so on will face monetary challenges – most of us are not exempt. Though for some, said challenges will be commonplace, hence seemingly inescapable. OK – enough with the doom and gloom rhetoric. Regarding financial duress – how does one overcome this? My response entails adherence to several principles. In this brief article, I will discuss one principle: acquiring knowledge.



knowledge: understand your spending habits



Shortly after finishing my MBA, I began repaying my dreaded student loan debt. Although I consolidated my loans, my monthly payment was a noticeable addendum to my monthly budget. I liken said addendum to a married couple forced to make room for an unwanted in-law seeking long-term residency.


In preparation for my pending unwanted resident, I put on my accountant hat and examined my monthly budget. I wanted to prepare myself for the worst possible financial scenario and devise strategies to overcome such adversity, versus later. Accordingly, a la the conservative accounting approach, I maxed out each expenditure on my spreadsheet. For example – on average, my light bill fell in the $100-$350 range. Historically, my light bill never peaked beyond $350. So, I entered LIGHTS at $350.


During my self-audit session, I had an epiphany. I discovered a spending habit that was inconspicuous. The habit or “objects of my desire” were snacks. To my chagrin, I hadn’t been intimately in tune with my spending habits.


I always paid my bills in full and on time. I never borrowed money from anyone. Additionally, each month, I had a fixed amount of funds deposited into my savings and stock brokerage accounts. Yet, something was amiss.


Per the findings of another Market Watch survey, conducted in May of 2024, over 66% of Americans live paycheck to paycheck. I have another disclaimer for you: I am not in the wealthy class. Also, I am not living paycheck to paycheck, for which I am blessed and grateful. Still, something wasn’t right. Soon I realized there was an elephant in the room. While tallying the transactions on snacks, the elephant grew. And then I saw how big the mammal was: In one month I had spent over $200 on SNACKS.


Had I not audited myself, I would have continued spending on junk food and other nonessentials. Small purchases are the silent assassins that wreak havoc in our wallets. In our psyche, we tell ourselves, “It’s just five bucks.” Yet, when we undervalue money – be it five dollars or one dollar – we develop habits to spend habitually on low-cost items. In the spirit of imparting knowledge, I’d like to reveal another threat – the cousin to small assasins. The threat in question is the subscription model. We’ve learned that small purchases appear harmless until one has accumulated a stack of transactions to prove otherwise. Yet, with subscriptions, these can be a dual threat:


1. Often, they’re small purchases.

2. They require one to subscribe – this is the hook.


Let’s revisit our psyche when dealing with small purchases – we tell ourselves, “It’s just five bucks.” But enter the hook, or tentacles that grasp your credit card for future payments. If you’ve committed to monthly self-audits, you’re fine. If you’re frugal by nature, don’t sweat it. If you check neither of the previously mentioned boxes, you’ll likely forget about your monthly subscriptions until financial duress forces you to make room for an unwanted resident. And yes, I, too, fell victim to the alluring trap of subscriptions.


So, other than monthly audits or frugality, there’s an alternative to managing your monthly subscriptions. First, thank God for apps! In a June 2024 CNBC article, writer Elizabeth Gravier suggests several apps to help users maintain cognizance of their monthly subscriptions. Each app is slightly different and tailored to users with different needs. I’m a proponent of technology – let it be your friend or resident.



Thanks for reading!

Joshua J. Lavallais
