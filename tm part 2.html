time management part 2

part 2: become efficient

Written by: Joshua J. Lavallais


Assuming you have audited your schedule, you should know how much spare time you have. Once you aim for a goal, you must quantify how much time is needed to reach your journey’s end. I recommend such for clarity and budgeting.




clarity




You must know that clarity yields the drive to complete all missions. Furthermore, when the necessary resources to fulfill your goal are quantifiable, there is a stronger sense of urgency to finish—mainly because you have constructed a timetable. Thus, the journey is a matter of staying the course until you achieve a predictable victory.


Case in point: As a teenager, I worked a full-time summer job in fast food. I loathed nearly every minute of it, but I wanted my first car, and I wanted it badly. I worked earnestly, sometimes well over 40 hours a week. I sacrificed most social activities to reach my quota of $1,600. Based on my hourly pay and the hours I worked each week, I knew approximately how long it would take to reach my magic number.


Here is another example: During graduate school, I listed every required class in a blank Word document. As I passed each course, I kept track by deleting each class from the list. The Word document began to shrink, and I felt myself getting closer and closer to graduation day. Then, one day, I deleted the last two classes from the list!


In both instances, as I inched closer to my goal, I worked harder.

Why?

Because the plans I set forth were unfolding. And with each incremental win, the smell of victory became stronger and less distant, and I grew more confident—without confidence, it's a wrap. Also, with confidence came momentum and determination to finish strong. Yes, it is possible to achieve gargantuan feats without clarity. Yet, with clarity, the journey is less rigid, while the goal is more palpable.





budgeting




Like budgeting money, budgeting your time can be difficult — sometimes, you cannot make do with the amount of free time you have. You must consider stretching your days when your calculated spare hours are not enough to realistically achieve your objectives. Stretching your days involves sacrificing sleep, amongst other activities. Wait — hold up. Before you agree to this, there is something called efficiency.


To be efficient means to accomplish goals without overusing or wasting resources. Within the context of this post, we will focus on “time” as our desired resource. To complete your goal, let us assume you must spend 50 hours within a two-week window, and your calculated total disposable time is 15 hours per week. Consequently, you are 20 hours short. To make up for the deficit, outside of stretching your days, what other options are there? Delegate tasks via outsourcing? Maybe—maybe not.




the pareto principle




More on delegation: it is common for individuals and small businesses to outsource jobs and responsibilities. One person can’t do everything, as there aren’t enough hours to complete all tasks within one day. An alternative to outsourcing is the Pareto Principle, also known as Pareto’s Law or the 80/20 Rule. Named after Italian engineer Vilfredo Pareto, the Pareto Principle recognizes that the majority of output comes from a minority of inputs, e.g., 20% of one’s customer base produces 80% of income earned – 20% of the clothes we have we wear 80% of the time – or 20% of one’s blog posts are read 80% of the time. Additionally, the Pareto Principle recommends prioritization to execute tasks that contribute to the majority gains sequentially.



Back to your time dilemma: despite having audited your schedule, you are short of time — we know this. Instead of working yourself to death or outsourcing to contractors, you chose the 80/20 rule. Successively, you did the following, just as I did on that slow workday:


1. Identified all activities of importance—those that will yield a higher return.

2. Arrange those tasks, from most important to least important, in this order.

3. Elected to spend more time on a few activities that would produce a more significant outcome.


The end result: You finished all tasks on time, without having wasted resources—this is efficiency.

Do all results/actions transpire in an 80/20 ratio, respectively?

No.

Sometimes the ratio is 90/10, 70/30, or 60/40. The goal is not to achieve a ratio of 80/20, results/actions, respectively. Instead, regarding the Pareto Principle, the goal is to maximize your output with minimal input.

Furthermore, as the ratios vary, so will the time allotted, as each activity will have a different duration. Let us assume you can achieve an outcome of 90% results and 10% action. If so, great! But concerning that 90/10 ratio, the endeavor is heavy: you are building a three-story house, brick by brick. Your minority input of 10%, although minor in the grand scheme, is still heavy, given the exceptional scope of work. Question: How do you prevent burnout, even when giving a mere tenth of your labor?
