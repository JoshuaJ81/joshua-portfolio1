time management part 1

part 1: take inventory

Written by: Joshua J. Lavallais


Time management is a skill set for implementing efficiency and order in place of chaos. Consider this: when engulfed in a flurry of deadlines while nowhere near completion, you may find yourself on the cusp of self-implosion. However, with proper planning and time management skills, one can mitigate and ultimately eliminate moments of panic.


Time management is indeed a system. And with the time management system comes the following ancillary practices:


1. Time inventory management .

2. How to be efficient.

3. How to increase productivity.


Before I proceed, I want to call a timeout.

Question: Is there a goal in your queue you have shirked because of little to zero time in your weekly schedule? If so, ponder how often you think about this goal. Gauge the practicality of this project coming to fruition. Keep this goal in mind as you read the remainder of the article.

Alright, let us resume.

···

I realize everyone’s time is limited—after all, life is short. A decade ago, I was 34 years old, but it feels like the last ten years of my life rushed onward at an unrecognizable speed.

Conversely, life is also LONG. I have endured moments that seemed to have paced along, just like a motorist who rides the brake while flashing their emergency blinkers. Life is long when you study for hours. And, too, life is long when you sit in traffic.

Short, long, or in between —how can you get the most out of your day? The answer: time management, of course. Other than a skill set, as noted previously, I see time management as a system. And while I have crafted systems to increase efficiency and productivity, I will not say that my system or any other system is the common denominator. Yet, whichever system you follow, taking inventory of your time should occur at the onset.




TIME MANAGEMENT TIP #1: TAKE INVENTORY OF YOUR TIME




Here is a hard fact: not everyone has time to spare. Dr. Donald E. Wetmore of the Productivity Institute states, "The average working person spends less than 2 minutes daily in meaningful communication with their spouse or significant other and less than 30 seconds daily in meaningful communication with their children.”


Superwomen are an exception, such as single mothers raising a family, working multiple jobs, and going to school. Also, some college students take a full course load while working full-time. Others join the ranks of men and women who barely have a minute to spare, but most people would be surprised to discover how much spare time they have if they logged their activity for one week.


Like your money, your time is a great asset and should be spent wisely and tracked accordingly. Just as you track monetary transactions in your bank account, you should track your time. If money, or lack of it, is a constant theme in your life, you may unknowingly spend recklessly on non-essential goods and services. If your favorite catchphrase is, “I don’t have time,” then maybe it’s time to track and analyze how you spend your minutes and hours.

···

Years ago, I tracked my activity for three hours on a slow workday. The day in question moved painfully slowly—yes just like the motorist who rides the brake while flashing their emergency blinkers. Surprisingly, I made an interesting discovery: I accomplished a lot.


How did I do it? How did I get a lot done? Before tracking my time, I turned to a concept called the Pareto Principle — said principle redefined my approach to work, as I learned how to work smarter and not harder. Later in the Part 2, I will discuss the Pareto Principle in depth and reveal how you can apply it to your work regimen.


More on that slow workday — as noted, I was amazed at the volume of work I did within a short time frame and on a light day. The experience in question illuminated the power of time and how a few hours are worth more than they appear on paper — seeing is believing. As you track your time daily, at work and in life, you will discover how much you do or how little you accomplish. Then, you can determine how much time you have wisely spent or how much time you have wasted.


Earlier, I asked you to consider a goal in your queue—let’s talk about it. Maybe you want to start a business? Lose weight? Gain muscle? Regardless, if you look at your daily activities and realize you spend hours watching Netflix, imagine what could happen if you devoted your time to your goal.


Realistically, you will not have time to design and build your dream life in one week. Yet even if, after analyzing your schedule, you realize that you have, say, one to three hours of disposable time per week, that’s great! The question is this: How can you use your spare time efficiently?
